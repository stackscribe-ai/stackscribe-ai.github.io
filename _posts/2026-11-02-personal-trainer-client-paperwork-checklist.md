Gyms and big studios hand new trainers a stack of client paperwork templates on day one. Independent trainers — the ones who go solo, train out of a home gym, outdoor space, or a client's garage — usually don't get that stack, and end up either skipping it entirely or improvising something in a Google Doc the night before a first session. Skipping it is the riskier of the two options: it's the paperwork that protects *you* if a client gets injured, disputes a charge, or no-shows repeatedly.

Here's what that stack should actually contain.

## 1. Client intake form

Basic contact info, training history, current activity level, and goals. This is the form that lets you actually program something appropriate instead of guessing — not a legal document, just the foundation everything else builds on.

## 2. PAR-Q (Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire)

A short screening questionnaire — heart conditions, chest pain, dizziness, bone/joint problems, medication, pregnancy — that flags whether a client should get a doctor's sign-off before starting an exercise program. The standard PAR-Q format is widely published and well-established in the fitness industry; the point isn't to diagnose anything yourself, it's to know when to say "get cleared by your doctor first" instead of guessing.

## 3. Liability waiver / assumption of risk

The document a client signs acknowledging that exercise carries inherent risk and that they're choosing to participate anyway. This is the one trainers most often skip because it feels adversarial to ask a new client to sign something about injury risk on day one — but it's exactly the document you want in place *before* anything goes wrong, not after.

## 4. Session agreement

What's actually being sold: session length, price, package terms, what's included (programming, in-session coaching, between-session check-ins) and what isn't. Without this in writing, scope creep ("can you also just quickly look at my diet?") and pricing disputes both get harder to resolve cleanly.

## 5. Cancellation / no-show policy

How much notice is required to cancel without charge, what happens on a no-show, and whether packages expire. This is the single most common source of awkward, unpaid conversations for solo trainers — having it in writing and referencing it (rather than negotiating it fresh every time) makes enforcing it far less personal.

## Plus: a tracker

Once you have clients signed up, you need somewhere to log sessions completed, package balances remaining, and renewal dates — otherwise "how many sessions does this client have left" turns into a memory exercise.
